# Traditional / Transitional

## What it is

Traditional interiors use symmetry, richer woods, tailored upholstery, and classic details (moldings, drapery, patterned textiles). Transitional softens that formality: cleaner lines, quieter patterns, and a bridge between classic and contemporary so the room feels timeless rather than period-costume.

## Do

- Favor symmetry in seating pairs, lamps, and art when the architecture supports it.
- Use warm woods (mahogany, cherry, walnut) or painted case goods with thoughtful hardware.
- Layer rugs, curtains, and upholstery; traditional rooms need textile depth.
- Transitional move: keep classic sofa shape but simplify legs/arms; reduce pattern count to one hero + solids.
- Palette: cream, navy, burgundy, forest, soft gold — or transitional neutrals with one deep accent.
- Lighting: chandelier or lantern as ambient + table lamps for pools of light.

## Don't

- Do not mix every pattern (floral + stripe + plaid + ikat) without a solid rest zone.
- Avoid ultra-minimal slab furniture as the only language if you want traditional warmth.
- Do not ignore scale: tiny lamps on huge case pieces read unfinished.
- Skip shiny chrome as the default metal unless transitional modern is the brief.

## Pairings and ratios

- Traditional: 60% quiet walls / 30% wood + patterned textile / 10% metallic or deep accent.
- Transitional: reduce pattern to 10–20% of the visual field; increase solid performance fabrics.
- Metals: aged brass or bronze dominant; nickel only if intentional and repeated.

## Room recipes

Living: tailored sofa, two matching chairs or one pair + ottoman, wood coffee table, lamps flanking a console. Dining: rectangular table, upholstered host chairs optional, sideboard, chandelier centered. Bedroom: upholstered or wood headboard, paired nightstands, layered bedding, rug under bed.

## Budget tradeoffs

Spend on sofa comfort and window treatments; they carry traditional rooms. Art and accessories can phase. One quality wood case piece beats three veneer fillers.

## Conflict watch

Traditional + industrial: keep wood furniture, swap crystal for metal pendants, add one raw material as accent only. Traditional + minimal: keep symmetry and textiles, strip ornament and reduce pattern to solids + one stripe.

## Pattern discipline

Traditional rooms collapse when pattern multiplies without a plan. Choose one hero pattern (rug or drapery or pillows — not all three competing). Surround with solids in the same temperature. Transitional rooms often replace damask with performance solids and a single stripe. If you inherit patterned chairs, calm the walls and rug. Scale patterns to the room: large rooms can take larger motifs; small rooms need quieter repeats.

## Formal vs everyday living

If the user wants traditional for guests but casual daily life, use durable performance fabric on the main sofa, keep the formal wood case piece, and let lamps and art carry polish. Avoid plastic slipcovers as the long-term answer — choose the right fabric once. Dining rooms can stay more formal than living rooms if cohesion is held by shared wood and metal.

## EXPLAIN anchors

Symmetry, warm wood, layered textiles, cream/navy/burgundy or transitional neutrals, chandelier-plus-lamps, pattern limited to one hero. Soften toward contemporary by simplifying legs and reducing pattern percentage.

## Field note

Re-check the room after each major purchase: does the latest piece reinforce the documented direction, or does it reopen a style conflict? If it reopens conflict, name the tension and re-assert dominant versus accent before buying further. Prefer one decisive correction over three small compromises that blur the plan. Keep shopping lists short: core pieces first, accents last, and only after the readiness of the room plan justifies them.
