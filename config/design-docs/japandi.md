# Japandi

## What it is

Japandi blends Japanese restraint with Scandinavian warmth: low furniture, natural materials, quiet palettes, and careful emptiness. It is not stark minimalism and not rustic cabin — it is calm craft with soft edges.

## Do

- Keep silhouettes low and horizontal; platforms, low sofas, and floor-near lighting feel right.
- Materials: oak or walnut (matte), linen, paper, ceramic, stone, rattan in small doses.
- Emphasize negative space; every object should earn its place.
- Use a limited palette: warm neutrals, soft black, clay, muted green, wood tones.
- Prefer handmade or imperfect textures (wabi-sabi) over glossy perfection.
- Storage should hide clutter; closed cabinets beat open chaos.

## Don't

- Do not overload with Scandinavian knits until the room feels cottage-cozy — Japandi needs quieter textile volume.
- Avoid bright primary colors and busy patterns as defaults.
- Do not mix many wood tones; one primary wood plus one accent max.
- Skip heavy traditional ornament and crystal sparkle as the main light language.

## Pairings and ratios

- 70% quiet surfaces / 20% wood + textile / 10% black or clay accent.
- Lighting: soft ambient + one paper lantern or linen shade; avoid harsh downlights alone.
- Greenery: one sculptural plant or branch arrangement beats a jungle.

## Room recipes

Living: low sofa, wood coffee table, closed media cabinet, paper pendant, large empty wall. Dining: simple table, bench or slim chairs, ceramic centerpiece only. Bedroom: platform bed, linen bedding, one lamp, minimal nightstand styling.

## Budget tradeoffs

Spend on the bed or sofa and one wood storage piece. Lighting and ceramics can phase later. Editing junk is free and does more than buying another accent.

## Conflict watch

Japandi + maximalist art walls: choose one feature wall max, keep other walls quiet. Japandi + industrial: allow black metal sparingly; keep surfaces warm and soft.

## Emptiness as a feature

Japandi fails when every surface hosts an object. Leave the coffee table mostly clear; style one tray max. Walls can stay empty longer than Western habits suggest — one strong art piece or none. Floor space in front of windows should read as breathable. If the user feels the room is "cold," add texture (linen, wool, ceramic) before adding count of objects. Warmth comes from material, not quantity.

## Kitchen and dining notes

Low open shelving with edited ceramics can work; crowded open shelves do not. Dining benches keep the horizontal line. Prefer matte tableware and wood or stone serving pieces over glossy sets. Pendant light should be soft and single, not a crystal cluster. Bridge to living areas by repeating the same wood tone and black accent.

## EXPLAIN anchors

Low silhouette, negative space, matte natural materials, quiet palette, closed storage, wabi-sabi texture. When mixed with maximalism, propose Japandi dominant shell with one accent wall of collected objects.

## Field note

Re-check the room after each major purchase: does the latest piece reinforce the documented direction, or does it reopen a style conflict? If it reopens conflict, name the tension and re-assert dominant versus accent before buying further. Prefer one decisive correction over three small compromises that blur the plan. Keep shopping lists short: core pieces first, accents last, and only after the readiness of the room plan justifies them.
