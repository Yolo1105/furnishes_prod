# Style Mixing Pitfalls

## The core rule

Mixing styles works when one language is dominant (~70%) and the second is accent (~30%). Two equal styles fight. Three strong languages usually collapse into clutter.

## Name the tension

When someone wants "minimalist but cozy maximalist shelves," say the conflict out loud: minimal volume vs collected display. Resolve with dominant/accent — e.g. minimal room shell, maximalist confined to one shelving wall — and lock that resolution as a preference.

## Common clashes

- Traditional ornament + ultra-minimal slab furniture with no textile bridge.
- Coastal soft blues + heavy industrial black metal everywhere.
- Scandi pale wood + dark walnut MCM case goods without a bridging mid-tone.
- Farmhouse rustic + glossy glam chrome and crystal as equals.
- Maximalist pattern on walls, sofa, and rug simultaneously with no solid rest.

## Fixes that usually work

- Pick dominant style; demote the other to lighting, art, or one furniture piece.
- Unify metals and wood undertones before adding more objects.
- Add a textile bridge (rug or curtains) that borrows from both palettes.
- Edit: remove the weakest third of accessories before buying a "bridging" piece.

## Do / Don't

Do decide dominant/accent in one sentence before shopping. Do repeat the accent style at least twice so it looks intentional. Don't silently average two styles into bland compromise without naming tradeoffs. Don't keep buying "a little of both" without removing conflicts.

## Worked examples

Minimal + cozy maximalist shelves: dominant minimal architecture and furniture; accent = one shelving elevation with collected objects; propose preference "minimal dominant / maximalist accent shelves." Japandi + industrial: Japandi materials and low forms dominant; one black metal lighting family as accent. Traditional + coastal: traditional symmetry and sofa comfort dominant; coastal palette and sheers as accent; drop heavy damask.

## Extraction and confirmation

When two styles appear in one message, Eva should name both, propose dominant/accent, and ask to confirm. Extraction should not silently keep only the first adjective. If the user later flips dominance, treat it as a style change, not a quiet overwrite.

## Shopping discipline while mixing

Buy the dominant-language anchors first (sofa, rug, main table). Add accent-language pieces last and only if the tension still reads intentional. If a cart contains equal counts from two styles, pause and re-state the ratio before checkout.

## EXPLAIN anchors

70/30 dominant/accent, name the tension, lock resolution as preference, unify metal/wood before more objects, edit before bridging purchases.

## Field note

Re-check the room after each major purchase: does the latest piece reinforce the documented direction, or does it reopen a style conflict? If it reopens conflict, name the tension and re-assert dominant versus accent before buying further. Prefer one decisive correction over three small compromises that blur the plan. Keep shopping lists short: core pieces first, accents last, and only after the readiness of the room plan justifies them.
