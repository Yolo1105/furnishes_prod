# Scandinavian

## What it is

Scandinavian interiors favor light, calm function: pale woods, white or soft gray walls, tactile textiles, and daylight as a design material. Hygge is warmth through texture and soft light — not cluttered coziness. The room should feel airy, honest, and easy to live in.

## Do

- Use birch, ash, pine, or light oak; keep wood pale and matte.
- Maximize natural light: sheer curtains, clear window sills, mirrors opposite windows when useful.
- Layer texture: wool throws, linen, knits, sheepskin accents, flatweave rugs — soft without visual noise.
- Choose simple silhouettes: straight sofa, light dining table, slim shelving.
- Add greenery and one or two handmade ceramics for life without clutter.
- Prefer white, off-white, soft gray, muted blue or sage as the palette spine.

## Don't

- Do not default to dark walnut everywhere; it reads heavier and less Scandi.
- Avoid ornate moldings, heavy drapes, and high-gloss lacquer as the main finish story.
- Do not fill every surface; negative space is part of the style.
- Skip harsh black-white high contrast as the only language — soften with wood and textile.

## Pairings and ratios

- 60% light walls and large surfaces / 30% pale wood + upholstery / 10% muted color or black metal detail.
- Lighting: warm white (2700–3000K), paper or frosted glass pendants, dimmers for evening hygge.
- Floor: light wood or soft gray textile; rugs should not fight the pale story.

## Room recipes

Living: light sofa, wool throw, pale coffee table, tall plant, simple pendant. Dining: light wood table, mix of simple chairs, linen runner. Bedroom: white bedding, pale wood nightstands, soft rug underfoot, minimal art.

## Small-space tips

Use multifunctional pieces (storage ottoman, wall shelves). Keep furniture legs visible. One large rug beats two small rugs that chop the floor.

## Budget tradeoffs

Invest in sofa comfort and good daylight control (sheers + blackout where needed). Accessories and plants stretch the look cheaply when silhouettes stay simple.

## Conflict watch

Scandi + maximalist shelves: keep room shell calm; confine collected objects to one vertical zone. Scandi + industrial: limit metal to lighting and frames so wood and textile stay primary.

## Daylight and evening modes

Scandinavian rooms succeed when daytime brightness and evening softness both work. Day: keep window treatments sheer or easily pushable; avoid stacking bulky furniture in front of glass. Evening: add at least two low lamps plus a dimmable pendant so the room does not rely on a single cool overhead. Candles or warm dimmers are part of the language, not decoration theater. If the apartment faces north and stays gray, push slightly warmer whites and add wood volume so the palette does not go clinical.

## Storage without visual noise

Closed storage is allowed and often necessary; the Scandi mistake is open shelves filled with mismatched packaging. Prefer cabinets with simple fronts, baskets that match, and one open shelf for books or ceramics. Entry benches with hidden storage and wall hooks in a single finish keep small homes calm. Kids' toys break the look fastest — plan a closed zone early.

## EXPLAIN anchors

Pale wood, white/gray spine, textile hygge, daylight first, negative space, and muted 10% color. If the user wants Scandi plus maximalist shelves, keep the shell Scandi and confine collections to one vertical band.

## Field note

Re-check the room after each major purchase: does the latest piece reinforce the documented direction, or does it reopen a style conflict? If it reopens conflict, name the tension and re-assert dominant versus accent before buying further. Prefer one decisive correction over three small compromises that blur the plan. Keep shopping lists short: core pieces first, accents last, and only after the readiness of the room plan justifies them.
