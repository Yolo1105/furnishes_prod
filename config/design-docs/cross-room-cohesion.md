# Cross-Room Cohesion

## What cohesion means

Homes feel calm when rooms share a related palette, metal story, and wood family — without being identical copies. Cohesion is a thread, not a uniform.

## Shared systems

- Pick a whole-home neutral (wall or trim family) and let rooms vary accent color within that temperature.
- Choose one dominant metal for hardware and lighting across adjacent rooms; allow a second metal as accent if repeated.
- Keep flooring continuous when possible; if materials change, use thresholds intentionally and echo tones.
- Repeat wood undertones (warm vs cool) so oak in the kitchen does not fight walnut in the living room without a bridge.

## Room-to-room recipes

- Open plan: use rugs and lighting to zone, but keep sofa fabric and dining wood in the same temperature family.
- Hallways: treat as connectors — same wall color or a transitional tone between rooms.
- Bedroom vs living: quieter saturation in sleep spaces; shared accent hue in smaller doses.

## Do / Don't

Do carry one accent color into at least two rooms. Do align curtain style or length language on the same facade of windows. Don't introduce a new style language in every doorway. Don't change metals at every threshold.

## When styles differ by room

Allowed if architecture or function differs (kids' room vs formal dining). Bridge with shared neutrals and one repeated material. Name the dominant style per room so Eva and the user do not silently contradict earlier choices.

## Open-plan zoning without style whiplash

Use rugs, pendant placement, and furniture backs to define kitchen, dining, and living — but keep the wood and metal story continuous. A navy living sofa can meet oak dining chairs if walls and floor stay related and accents repeat. Avoid a farmhouse kitchen island colliding with a glam living room chrome set unless you intentionally design a bridge material (shared black metal or shared warm wood).

## Renovation phasing

If floors change later, pick a temporary rug strategy that already matches the target undertone. Paint is cheap cohesion: a shared trim white across rooms buys time while furniture arrives in stages. Confirm the whole-home metal early so hardware purchases do not drift.

## Talking to the user

Ask which room is the "hero" visitors see first; prioritize cohesion from that sightline. Secondary rooms can vary accents more freely. Document the shared systems (neutral, metal, wood) as preferences so later recommendations stay consistent.

## EXPLAIN anchors

Shared neutral and metal, related wood undertones, continuous flooring when possible, accent color echoed across rooms, bridges when styles differ by function.

## Field note

Re-check the room after each major purchase: does the latest piece reinforce the documented direction, or does it reopen a style conflict? If it reopens conflict, name the tension and re-assert dominant versus accent before buying further. Prefer one decisive correction over three small compromises that blur the plan. Keep shopping lists short: core pieces first, accents last, and only after the readiness of the room plan justifies them.
